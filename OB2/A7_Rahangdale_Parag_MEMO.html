The Burning Platform
A 2-Year Window.
Miss It, Lose Them Forever.

Broadcom's acquisition of VMware reset the entire enterprise infrastructure market — and opened a narrow, time-critical window for Nutanix to win.

2
Years until legacy

The Trigger
Broadcom Acquires VMware — and Kills Legacy Pricing
Post-acquisition, Broadcom immediately restructured VMware licensing, eliminating perpetual deals and forcing customers onto opaque, expensive subscription bundles.
The Window
2-Year Migration Decision Point
Enterprise customers faced a binary choice when their legacy deals expired: absorb Broadcom's new pricing or migrate now to an alternative. This was Nutanix's only window — once decided, customers don't switch again.
The Stakes
$50–100M ARR per Deal. Fortune 500 Infrastructure.
These were not small accounts — each migration was a Fortune 500 infrastructure contract worth $50–100M ARR. A delayed tool meant customers defaulted back to VMware. There was no second chance.

I apply all three lenses to explain why the in-place conversion project failed to execute — and what must change.

Architecture

Misaligned
Incentives &
Structural Holes

  • No single DRI for end-to-end customer outcome — each team had separate manager, roadmap & backlog
  • Engineers incentivized on functional stability, not whether the full conversion workflow worked for the customer
  • Cross-team interface decisions required multi-layer escalations adding weeks to hour-level problems
  • Handoff specs defined unilaterally by each team — never co-owned
05 / 12 Analysis 1 of 3
Root Cause
Structural holes existed between the 4 teams. No bridging role, no shared planning forum, no integrated backlog. Each team was an information island — rational behaviour for individuals trapped in a functional incentive structure.
Key Concept
Structural holes: gaps in information flow between otherwise dense but siloed networks — the "blind men and the elephant" problem.
Culture

The Assumption
That Silence
Is Safe

  • Move team repeatedly discovered AHV/Networking incompatibilities weeks into a sprint — blockers not surfaced early
  • Retrospectives happened after delays — not during the work
  • Teams defaulted to async comms (JIRA, email) for decisions requiring synchronous resolution
  • Implicit belief: exposing a dependency on another team signals weakness
06 / 12 Analysis 2 of 3
Root Cause
A culture of "hiding problems is safe" — reinforced daily by review rituals, meeting formats and solo accountability metrics. Habits, not values, locked in the silence.
NUMMI Principle
"You cannot change culture by telling people to think differently. You change it by changing what people do every day."
Power & Influence

Responsibility
Without
Authority

  • Move team accountable for delivering the product — zero formal authority over the 3 teams it depended on
  • Conversion interface decisions blocked in competing backlog prioritization meetings controlled by functional managers
  • AHV & Networking treated the conversion project as a secondary dependency on their own roadmaps
  • Move team relied entirely on relational influence and informal persuasion — no structural lever to escalate
07 / 12 Analysis 3 of 3
Root Cause
Each team operated in a closed network — dense internally, sparse externally. No open-network brokers existed to bridge information across team lines, fuelling political paralysis at every handoff.
Network Concept
Closed vs. open network topology: information hoarding reinforces existing power structures and starves cross-functional coordination of the data it needs.
08 / 12 Recommendations
Three Interventions

One Integrated Solution

01
Architecture
Cross-Functional Squad with Shared OKRs
Form a dedicated squad — 1 lead from each of the 4 teams + a single Product DRI with authority over the end-to-end customer outcome. Replace functional KPIs with shared metrics: time-to-convert, success rate, NPS.
Closes structural holes; forces open networks by design
02
Culture
Stop-the-Line Blocker Ritual
Any team member can and must flag a cross-team blocker in a shared weekly War Room — 30 min synchronous huddle. Blockers treated as collective problems. Draws directly from the NUMMI andon cord principle.
Changes daily behaviour → changes culture (NUMMI)
03
Power & Influence
Broker Roles & Shared Transparency
Formally recognize cross-functional connectors who already bridge teams informally — give them a mandate. Deploy a unified dashboard: shared dependency map, blocker log, milestone tracker visible to all 4 teams.
Removes information hoarding; shifts power to open networks
09 / 12 Implementation
Sequencing for Change

Not a Big Bang —
A Test-and-Learn Cycle

Phase 1
Weeks 1–4 · Foundation
Build Coalition & Launch Pilot
  • Identify existing cross-functional connectors (the "unsung influencers")
  • Form one pilot squad around one customer journey (ESXi → AHV mid-market cluster)
  • Launch shared dashboard immediately — quick win, low resistance
Phase 2
Weeks 5–12 · Momentum
Prove Value & Build the Case
  • Introduce Stop-the-Line ritual with pilot squad
  • Demonstrate early wins: shorter dependency-resolution time, one on-schedule conversion
  • Use pilot data to make business case for broader rollout
Phase 3
Month 4+ · Scale
Institutionalise & Reward
  • Extend squad model to all 4 team interfaces
  • Revise incentive structures with HR — tie manager reviews to cross-functional outcomes
  • Formalize broker roles in job descriptions
⚡ Managing Resistance: Functional VPs may resist loss of roadmap control. Use agenda-linking — frame the squad model as protecting their team's reputation by reducing blame-shifting and re-work.
10 / 12 Bullet-Proofing
Anticipating the Obstacles

Risks & Mitigation

Risk Likelihood Mitigation
Role confusion — dual reporting in pilot squad Medium Define "rules of the road": squad owns what, functions own how
Functional VPs resist shared OKRs (loss of autonomy) High Agenda-link: shared OKRs protect each team from blame for project failures

12 / 12 Conclusion
The Root Was Never the Technology

The DNA Was the Problem.
The DNA Is the Solution.

Nutanix's in-place hypervisor conversion project failed to execute on time because its DNA — architecture, culture, and power structure — was built for functional excellence, not cross-functional delivery.

We had the right engineers, the right vision, the right customer need. What was missing was an organizational system designed to let four teams move as one.

"You don't change culture by changing minds. You change it by changing structures, behaviours, and daily rituals — then watch the mindset follow."
